Abstract
Lately, improving the efﬁciency of human-robot interaction (HRI) has gained signiﬁcant importance, particularly in industrial
settings, where the robot’s ability to understand and act on human gestures has become essential for a smooth collaboration.
This paper contributes to the challenge of real-time gesture recognition for mobile robot teleoperation. It introduces a new
tailored dataset GESTRO with 11 dynamic gestures captured in 3,300 samples in total dedicated for intuitively controlling
mobile autonomous systems. Based on the proposed dataset, this paper introduces a LSTM (long short-term memory) model
with attention mechanism, achieving an accuracy of 0.98. An extensive evaluation examines the data quality and the importance
of dynamic sequence for accurate gesture detection. Finally, the proposed model is implemented on a mobile robotics system,
demonstrating its usability in realistic human-robot interaction setting. The proposed dataset along with the presented models
are made publicly available ( https://github.com/thohemp/gestro).
Keywords Gesture recognition · Human robot interaction · Keypoint detection · Dynamic gestures · Robot teleoperation
1 Introduction
Human-robot interaction has emerged as a key area of
robotics research, dedicated to facilitating seamless and
intuitive exchanges between robots and human operators.
This includes establishing intuitive communication chan-
nels [ 43], especially non-contact ones [ 42, 43]a sw e l la s
capturing important social cues [ 15] and signals [ 2]. The

robots to understand and adapt to human behavior, thereby
facilitating seamless collaboration and interaction in vari-
ous domains, ranging from industrial [ 36, 47] and medical
automation [12] to healthcare assistance [ 19, 46], domestic
robotics [39, 41] and service applications [ 16, 30]. Gesture
recognition can serve as a critical component in bridging the
gap between human commands and robotic actions [ 35, 38].
However, this is challenging due to the complex and dynamic
environmental conditions in which human-robot interac-
tions typically occur. V arying lighting, background clutter,
occlusions, and user-speciﬁc variations impose signiﬁcant
demands on recognition systems [ 10, 49]. By decoding the
intricate language of gestures, robots can discern human
intentions, enabling them to perform tasks effectively and

2 Related Work
The task of dynamic gesture recognition, as opposed to
static gesture recognition, involves analyzing poses over
time, capturing the motion and transitions between different
states. This distinction is crucial as it introduces a temporal
dimension to gesture recognition, adding complexity but also
enhancing the system’s ability to interpret more nuanced and
natural human commands.
2.1 Gesture Datasets
Over the recent years, numerous gesture datasets have been
proposed and made publicly available. These datasets dif-
fer in the type of gesture, whether static or dynamic, their
purpose and the selected gesture classes, as well as in size
in terms of the number of samples and classes. Table 1 pro-
vides an overview of recent popular datasets, including a
description of their key attributes. The largest dataset was
presented by Materzynsk et al. [ 27], called Jester, including
148,092 samples, distributed across 27 classes. The samples
were collected from 1,300 actors from close range in front of
a notebook camera using static hand gestures. The selected
classes may serve as general-purpose gestures; however, the
dataset’s capturing setup and static gestures make it unsuit-
able for robot interaction purposes. Similarly, the datasets
presented in [ 13, 24, 48, 52] provide large amounts of data,
but with gestures that are not intuitively used in human-robot
interactions.
In their study, Laplaza et al. [ 22] presented a data set
designed to control a social mobile robot. They provide static
and dynamic gestures that are employed for fundamental
teleoperation commands (e.g.turn left, turn right) and, more-
over, for communication purposes (e.g.,approve and conﬁrm
understanding). However, the dataset is limited in size, com-
prising only 450 samples, and is therefore not suitable for
learning-based recognition methods.
This paper bridged this gap by introducing a new dataset
with eleven universally dynamic gestures targeting the con-
trol of mobile robots for elementary teleoperation. It consists
of 3,300 samples equally balanced across all classes, provid-
ing double the amount of data compared to similar datasets,
such as those referenced in [ 21] and [ 45]. Along this paper
it will be demonstrated that this data is sufﬁcient in order to
be used in trainable gesture recognition approaches.
2.2 Gesture Recognition
In the recent literature, a common approach for gesture recog-
nition is CNNs (Convolutional Neural Networks) to extract
the meaningful features from the image data for classiﬁca-
tion [ 17, 20, 34, 51]. Most of these are constructed to only
process a single frame, thus, limited to solely static gesture
detection. Laplaza et al. [ 22] proposed a landmark-based
approach using MediaPipe [ 25] to predict 33 body land-
marks along with their estimated depth positions based on
RGB images. Despite providing sequences for the dynamic
gestures in their dataset, their classiﬁcation model only takes
the last frame of each sample into account. Therefore, only
static gestures are recognized and classiﬁed by a four fully
connected layer network.

To detect dynamic gestures and facilitate the temporal
dimension, other methods [ 3, 28, 45] use 3D-CNNs that are
capable of processing a series of frames. Depending on the
number of frames, this approach is computational expen-
sive and can impair real-time detection on mobile systems.
In the search for more efﬁcient encoding approaches, land-
mark extraction strategies became a popular choice [ 1, 7,
26]. Ansar and Ahmad [ 5] detect hand key points that are
fed into an RNN (Recurrent Neural Network) for spatial per-
ception of dynamic gestures. Similarly, Samaan et al. [ 37]
additionally track head and body pose to capture sign ges-
tures from dynamic sequences based on an RNN. To improve
generalization, Zeghoud et al.[ 50] used hand landmarks and
proposed an additional spatial normalization layer that can be
equipped on various model types. They tested their method
on RNN, LSTM and GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit) architec-
tures.
In the following, a new approach adapting the idea of land-
mark encoding in combination with Bidirectional LSTM is
proposed to detect and classify dynamic gestures on mobile
platforms. The proposed LSTM is equipped with an addi-
tional attention layer, that allows capturing different spacial
relationships between the joints simultaneously, allowing
to generate a more profound understanding of the pose
changes while performing the gestures. At the same time,
the computational effort remains limited, leading to real-time
processing capabilities on mobile robotic platforms.
3 Dataset
Due to the lack of suitable dynamic gesture datasets tailored
for intuitive commanding of mobile robots, a new dataset for
teleoperating mobile platforms is introduced. In the process
of selecting the commands and their corresponding gestures,
ten fundamental and universally applicable classes are iden-
tiﬁed that are essential for teleoperating mobile robots. In
addition to these ten classes, another class for unknown ges-
tures is added that serves as a rest class. It allows the handling
of non-gestures or gestures that are not targeted towards the
robot and, therefore, reduces false positive classiﬁcations.
The selected commands are displayed in Table 2.T h e y
include basic teleoperation commands such as Move F or-
ward, Move Backward, Move Left, Move Right, Stop and
security commands such asLogin, Logout to restrict access to
the gesture recognition system to system administrators only.
Additionally, more complex, but fundamental commands
with F ollowand Go to Base complete the selection. For each
command, intuitive and easily distinguishable gestures were
chosen as shown in Fig. 1.

3.1 Data Acquisition
For data collection, a diverse group of ten participants was
selected, varying in race, age, gender, stature and background
(see Fig. 2). Each participant performed each of the ten ges-
tures 30 times and was recorded by a laptop camera with an
image resolution of 800 × 600 pixels. Every atomic gesture
is encapsulated in 30 frames, which, based on the camera’s
frame rate, equals to 1.5 seconds. Each of these recordings
serves as single samples. The records for the unknown class
were captured based on random videos from Y ouTube with
persons recorded in a similar setup. There are 300 samples
for every class, resulting in a dataset of 3,300 samples in
total.
3.2 Data Preprocessing
The dataset preprocessing involves the extraction of human
pose landmarks (Fig. 3). To this end, a robust pose esti-
mator is selected that is trained on the COCO-WholeBody
dataset [ 18] and consists of a ResNet152 backbone and a
heatmap prediction head. It simultaneously estimates 133
2d-landmarks for the entire body, including three landmarks
for each foot, 21 landmarks for each hand, 59 face landmarks
and 17 body landmarks. From this set of keypoints those from
the upper body are selected, including face, arms and hands,
resulting in a subset of 54 landmarks, as shown in Fig. 4.
The remaining 79 landmarks are omitted since they are not
involved during the execution of the chosen gestures.

the frame’s size, resulting in 2D landmarks positions with

5 Experiments
This section describes the training and experiments of the
proposed model GESTRO-LSTM on our proposed GESTRO
dataset. It starts with a description of the experimental
setup, followed by an introduction to the evaluation metrics.
Multiple experiments are conducted including the compar-
ison of the proposed model architecture with other similar
architectures, class-wise performance evaluations and further
analysis of regarding the impact of the sequence length on
the classiﬁcation results. The section closes with the imple-
mentation and initial test of the proposed model on a real
humanoid robot.
5.1 Experimental Setup
The dataset was divided into a training set (80%, subject 1-8)
and a test set (20%, subject 9-10) for the purpose of model
training. The division was made with respect to the subjects,
using the data of eight subjects for training and the data of
the remaining two subjects for testing. This way, the model
cannot learn the individual joint placements of the test sub-
jects, that could occur in a randomly mixed split. The model
Fig. 6 Loss and accuracy curves over the training epochs of our pro-
posed GESTRO-LSTM model on the GESTRO dataset
was trained for 30 epochs using the Adam optimizer [11] and
a learning rate of 0.0001. Figure 6 illustrates the trends in the
loss and accuracy curves for the training and test sets over the
epochs. It demonstrates that the model can learn quickly and
starts to converge at epoch seven. The jumps in the test loss
and test accuracy curves are caused by the relatively small
number of test samples.
5.2 Evaluation Metrics
The evaluation of the GESTRO-LSTM model on our pro-
posed GESTRO dataset was performed using multiple per-
formance metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, and
F1 score. These metrics provide complementary insights:
Accuracy provides an overall measure of correctness, pre-
cision evaluates the model’s ability to avoid false positives,
recall highlights its ability to identify true positives, and the
F1 score balances these two aspects. The analysis was per-
formed over individual training epochs to observe learning
trends and to detect overﬁtting. A comparative evaluation
against different methods was also performed to highlight
their respective strengths and weaknesses. In addition, inter-
class comparisons were performed to assess class sensitivity
and class-wise robustness, ensuring a detailed understanding
of the model’s behavior across different categories.

5.5 One-vs-All
In the prior experiments, the ﬁrst eight subjects’ data was
used for training, while the remaining data was used for test-
ing. To conﬁrm that the observed data quality applies to the
entire datasets, a one-vs-all test is performed. For this, the
data of one subject is used as a test set, while the data for the
other nine subjects is used for training. The results in Table 6
show that the strong performance is effectively maintained
throughout the variation between individuals. This also indi-
cates that our proposed model can adapt to different subjects
without being biased by unique features that could signiﬁ-
cantly degrade classiﬁcation performance. This suggests that
the model has identiﬁed relevant features and patterns that
remain stable across subjects. As a result, the model is well-
equipped to perform reliably on unseen data, making it highly
robust and suitable for real-world applications where new,
unknown subjects are encountered.

6 Robot Implementation
To assess the efﬁcacy of the proposed methodology in a
real-world setting, the GESTRO-LSTM and the Transformer
model were deployed on the humanoid robot TIAGo, man-
ufactured by PAL Robotics [ 32]( s e eF i g .11). In this setup,
frames were processed without a queue, i.e. frames arriv-
ing faster than the system could process were skipped. This
approach ensured the real-time capability of the system,
but also increased the difﬁculty of processing the tempo-
ral dynamics of gestures, which signiﬁcantly challenged the
robustness of the models. Under these real-world conditions,
the Transformer exhibited greater robustness problems than
expected based on its dataset performance. In contrast, the
GESTRO-LSTM showed strong robustness in gesture recog-
nition. The transition between gestures usually required a
short period of time to build up the necessary probability
threshold. However, the safety-critical stop gesture was rec-
ognized immediately, highlighting its distinction from other
gestures. In addition, the unknown gesture effectively cap-
tured outliers, whether they were gestures foreign to the
dataset or arbitrary movements, demonstrating its strong gen-
eralization ability.
The latency evaluation on the real robot’s Jetson AGX Orin
revealed an FPS throughput of 29.94 for published images,
while the pose estimation node operated at 6.972 Hz and the
gesture recognition node at 4.81 Hz. The initial detection
latency of 5.528 s for the ﬁrst gesture can be attributed to
the LSTM model, which requires 30 frames to be processed
before producing its ﬁrst prediction, and therefore does not
reﬂect the system’s steady-state responsiveness. A more rep-
resentative measure is the latency from the ﬁrst image to the
ﬁrst gesture change, which was measured at 2.519 s. This
indicates that once the LSTM buffer is ﬁlled, the system
is capable of detecting gesture transitions within a reason-
able timeframe. In addition, the execution of the gestures
themselves introduces a temporal component, as a complete
gesture typically spans several frames (e.g., roughly 0.7 to
2.5 seconds depending on the gesture, multiplied by the num-
ber of performed repetitions), which is inherently included
in the measured latency. Consequently, once the system has
observed a gesture, subsequent gestures can be recognized
very quickly, in most cases even before the gesture has fully
completed. Overall, the real-time performance on the phys-
ical robot demonstrates that the gesture recognition pipeline
operates at a sufﬁcient rate for interactive applications, with
the steady-state latency being well within acceptable bounds.
7 Limitations
While the proposed model demonstrates strong performance
on the test set, the real-world experiments on the humanoid
robot revealed that the pose prediction is sensitive to varia-
tions in viewing angles and environmental conditions. These
factors can negatively impact the quality of the extracted
motion data and therefore deteriorate the gesture classiﬁca-
tion. To address this issue, introducing increased landmark
noise during training may enhance the model’s robustness
and improve the robustness of the gesture classiﬁcation for
pose prediction in real-world settings. In addition to these
limitations, the current method necessitates supervised train-
ing for each novel gesture category and does not support
zero-shot learning. This limitation imposes constraints on
scalability in practical applications where the rapid integra-
tion of new gestures is imperative. Subsequent research could
entail the exploration of integrating semantic gestures for
zero-shot learning. Moreover, although the dataset was col-
lected from participants varying in race, age, gender, and
stature, the total number of ten participants remains relatively
small. This limited participant count may introduce demo-
graphic bias, as certain body proportions, movement styles,
or cultural gesture tendencies may be underrepresented.
While our one-vs-all evaluation (Table6) demonstrates stable
performance across all subjects, a broader participant pool
including a wider range of age groups, physical abilities, and
cultural backgrounds would further strengthen the general-
izability of the model. Future data collection efforts should
aim to increase both the number and diversity of participants
to mitigate potential biases and ensure robust performance
across a wider population.
8 Conclusion
Here’s the updated conclusion: This paper presents a new
dataset GESTRO for the recognition of gestures in tele-
operated mobile robotics and a novel gesture recognition
model GESTRO-LSTM for the efﬁcient interaction between
humans and robots. Further, it proposes an implementation to
demonstrate the practical utility and real-time functionality
of the system.
The introduced GESTRO dataset contains 3300 samples
of dynamic gestures split into 11 classes, which have been
used to transmit basic instructions to mobile systems. The
dataset constitutes a valuable contribution to the existing cor-
pus of data on gesture recognition, given the diversity of the
test subjects and the extensive range of gestures captured. Its
public availability will facilitate further developments in this
ﬁeld.
The presented model employs an LSTM model with an
attention mechanism for the nuanced interpretation of ges-
tures, achieving a competitive test accuracy of 98% and
remarkable robustness across all target classes. These results,
combined with the model’s lightweight architecture and
real-time processing capability, make it particularly suited

support its applicability in settings with varying operators

workﬂows, gesture-based teleoperation offers a natural and
efﬁcient communication channel that can complement exist-

human-robot collaboration.
9 Future Work
Future work will focus on further testing the implemented
system in unconstrained environments to evaluate its prac-
tical applicability and reliability in dynamic, real-world
situations. More precisely, we will integrate the proposed
method into an autonomous mobile robot designed for pallet
transportation, where human operators remotely control the
robot to operate within designated target areas. The study
will demonstrate the model’s ability to detect gestures effec-
tively in real, cluttered environments and will provide useful
insights for enhancing the current system.
